Privacy

What each part of Rowel can see, stated precisely enough to be checked against the source.

Last updated 15 August 2026.

The short version

  • There are no accounts. We never ask for your name, email, or phone number.
  • Your conversations, code, and commands are encrypted between your phone and your Mac. The relay cannot decrypt them.
  • The relay keeps no logs. What little it does store — which machines are online, and a pairing code while you are using it — is deleted the moment it stops being needed.
  • There is no analytics SDK, no crash reporter, and no advertising or tracking code in the app.
  • On the same Wi-Fi, nothing touches the relay at all.

What the relay can see

The app and Bridle perform a Noise IK handshake with each other and exchange sealed frames. The relay switches those frames between two sockets by circuit number. It has no key material and cannot read the contents.

It does necessarily observe:

Machine identifier A random per-machine ID generated when Bridle is installed. It is not derived from your hardware and is not linked to you.
Machine name The display name Bridle reports, so the app can show you which Mac you are about to connect to. This is in the clear, and it defaults to your computer's name — edit machineName in ~/.rowel/bridle.json if that name says more about you than you would like.
Bridle version Reported at registration.
Who is connected to whom That some phone has an open circuit to some machine, and when it opened.
Traffic volume and timing Unavoidable for anything that forwards bytes. Enough to tell that you are busy, not what you are doing.
Network address Your IP address, as with any server you connect to.

It never sees: your prompts, the model's replies, file contents, diffs, command output, your Harness's address, your API keys, or your file paths.

During pairing

While a pairing code is outstanding — at most 15 minutes, and usually a few seconds — the relay holds the pairing bundle so the app can fetch it with the short code. That bundle contains your machine's name, its public key, its local network addresses, and a one-time token that can only ask to pair — someone at the Mac has to accept it. It is dropped as soon as the code is claimed or expires.

What the relay stores, and for how long

The relay runs on Cloudflare's edge, as a set of small objects each holding one connection or one pairing code. Two of them write to durable storage, and both delete what they wrote as soon as it stops being needed:

While a Mac is online One row: its machine ID, display name, Bridle version, and when it connected. Deleted when it disconnects.
While a pairing code is outstanding The pairing bundle, so the app can fetch it with the code. Deleted the moment the code is claimed, or when it expires — at most 15 minutes.

Nothing else is written anywhere. Not your messages, not who talked to whom, not how much, not when. There is no log file, no analytics pipeline, and no backup — the objects that carry your traffic hold no storage at all, and the frames passing through them are sealed anyway.

Two consequences, one good and one worth knowing: we could not hand over your history because we never had it; and the relay has no memory of you between sessions, so nothing accumulates.

Cloudflare

The relay runs at rowel-relay.novabox.ai, and since August 2026 it is a Cloudflare Worker rather than a program on a machine we rent. It has a hostname of its own, separate from this site, so that a rule aimed at these pages cannot reach the thing your phone depends on. Cloudflare therefore both terminates TLS and runs the code, and can observe the same metadata described above — your IP address, timing, and volume — subject to its own privacy policy. It cannot read your traffic: what it forwards is already sealed between your phone and your Mac.

Being honest about the shape of that: running on someone else's edge removes a server we could misconfigure and adds a company that could be compelled. The end-to-end encryption is what makes both of those bounded — neither we nor Cloudflare can produce your conversations, because neither has ever held them in a readable form.

What stays on your devices

On the Mac

Bridle keeps its identity key and its list of paired phones under ~/.rowel. Your conversations stay wherever the DeepSeek Harness already keeps them; Bridle does not copy them anywhere.

On the phone

Less than you might expect. The app keeps its identity key in the iOS Keychain, and four small things in its own sandboxed storage: the list of paired Macs, the name this iPhone reports, which Mac it was last on, and the model new conversations should start with.

Your conversations are not stored on the phone at all. The session list and every message are fetched from your Mac when you open the app and held only in memory, so closing it discards them. That is a consequence of the Mac being the only source of truth rather than a privacy feature we are claiming credit for — but the effect is the same, and it is worth knowing that a phone with Rowel on it is not carrying a copy of your work.

Unpairing a Mac in the app's settings is one-sided: the phone forgets that machine, but the Mac still lists the phone until you run bridle revoke there. We could have made the app ask the Mac to forget it, and chose not to — a phone that wants to be forgotten is exactly the phone whose word should not be taken for it. Run bridle devices on the Mac to see what it still trusts.

The lock on the app

Rowel locks itself, because a paired iPhone can approve commands on your Mac and your phone being unlocked in someone else's hands is the gap the system lock does not cover. It asks for Face ID, Touch ID, or your passcode on launch and after an idle timeout you choose, and it covers the screen the moment the app stops being frontmost — so the app switcher photographs a lock screen rather than your work.

It bounds the window; it does not close it. If you lose the phone, the thing that actually revokes its access is bridle revoke on the Mac, and there is no way to do that from another phone yet.

Notifications

When your agent stops to ask you something and the app is not running, the machine has the relay ring your phone. Only Apple can wake a suspended iOS app, and Apple only accepts a push signed with a developer key, so the relay is the one part of this that signs — and therefore the one part that learns a device token.

The push carries no content. The words in the banner are a fixed sentence written into the relay's source, and the message the machine sends the relay has no field an agent's question could go in. There is nothing to leak rather than a promise not to look. Your phone wakes, opens its own encrypted tunnel to your machine, asks what happened, and writes the real notification locally — text that never crossed the relay and never reached Apple.

What this does mean, stated plainly:

  • The relay learns your device token at the moment a push is sent. It is handed over one wake at a time by your machine and is not stored.
  • Apple sees the machine's display name. The banner reads “Your agent is waiting on you · name”, so that someone with two Macs knows which one stopped. That name, the timing, and your device token are what Apple's push service handles.
  • Apple's push service therefore knows that a machine wanted your attention, and when. It does not know what about.
  • Your machine rings every phone you have paired with it that has notifications switched on — it does not try to guess which one you are holding.

Turning notifications off in iOS Settings is enough to stop it: the app notices on its next launch and tells the machine to forget the token.

Children

Rowel is a developer tool and is not directed at children under 13.

Changes

Material changes will be reflected here with a new date at the top. Because the relay keeps no record of who uses it, there is no mailing list to notify — checking this page is the only mechanism, which is a real limitation of collecting nothing.

Checking any of this

The claims above are structural, so they can be verified rather than trusted. The relay's entire state is in relay/src/registry.ts and relay/src/offers.ts, and a test named the relay only ever sees ciphertext asserts the central claim on every run.
